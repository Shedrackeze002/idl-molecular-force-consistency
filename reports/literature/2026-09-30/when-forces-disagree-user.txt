011                                                A BSTRACT
012
013            Direct-force neural network potentials (NNIPs) offer superior speed for atomistic
014            simulations, but their reliability is limited by the lack of a fast and data-free uncer-
015            tainty estimate to monitor the impact of non-conservativity and prediction errors.
016            While ensembles are data-free but slow, and other single-model methods often re-
017            quire training data, we introduce an approach that combines the advantages of both.
018
               Our metric is derived from the internal disagreement between a model’s directly
               predicted force and its energy-gradient-derived force, motivated by our finding that
019
               a model’s internal self-consistency is more critical for algorithmic stability than
020
               its external accuracy. We then identify an asymmetric failure mode inherent to
021            the direct-force architecture that this metric can diagnose, and also show a strong
022            monotonic correlation between the disagreement and the true force error across
023            diverse materials and out-of-distribution structures. We propose the link between
024            internal disagreement and practical reliability is a consequence of inter-head influ-
025            ence via the shared graph neural network embedding. We provide direct evidence
026            for this mechanism by showing that fine-tuning the conservative force pathway on
027            adversarial data that maximizes this internal disagreement measurably improves
028            the stability of simulations driven only by the direct force. The metric serves as
029
               a versatile and out-of-the-box tool that is competitive with expensive ensembles,
               offering both an on-the-fly assessment of model reliability and a principled method
030
               for generating targeted data to improve the stability of direct-force models.

034   1   I NTRODUCTION
035
036   Direct-force neural network interatomic potentials (NNIPs) are increasingly favored for their com-
037   putational efficiency in large-scale atomistic simulations (Gasteiger et al., 2021; Liao et al., 2024;
038   Neumann et al., 2024; Rhodes et al., 2025). This speed, however, comes at the cost of reliability. By
039
      decoupling the force prediction from a scalar potential, direct-force models are not guaranteed to
      be energy-conserving, leading to known algorithmic instabilities in molecular dynamics (MD) (Bigi
040
      et al., 2025) and poor performance in property prediction tasks that depend on the potential energy
041
      surface (PES) curvature (Póta et al., 2024; Loew et al., 2025). Hybrid integration schemes like
042   Multiple-Time-Stepping (MTS), where conservative forces are used to correct direct forces at a
043   certain frequency during simulations, have been shown to successfully stabilize the simulations while
044   mostly recover the speed of direct forces (Bigi et al., 2025). However, even with such schemes, a lack
045   of a fast and effective metric to monitor a model’s reliability (e.g., the impact of non-conservativity
046   and prediction errors) in real-time still remains.
047
      Current uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods present a difficult trade-off for developing such a
048   metric: model ensembles are data-free but computationally prohibitive, while faster single-model
049   methods often require access to the original training data. This work aims develop a universal
050   monitoring metric for direct-force models that combines the advantages of both paradigms. To do
051   so, we first investigate the fundamental principles that govern simulation stability since direct-force
052   models are known for its unstability (Fu et al., 2024; Bigi et al., 2025). While the work of Fu et al.
053   (2023; 2024) established that static force accuracy is an insufficient metric for dynamics and proposed
      that conservativity are one of the key requirements for reliable NNIPs, the relative importance of


                                                          1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




054
      conservativity compared to accuracy has not been directly demonstrated. Our investigation leads to a
055   series of discoveries that provide this missing evidence.
056
057   We first provide empirical proof that a model’s internal self-consistency is more critical for algorithmic
058
      stability than its external accuracy, confirming and building upon the principles laid out by Fu et al.
      (2023; 2024). This finding motivates our use of an internal disagreement metric, the Force Delta
059
060
      (U∆ ), which is the difference between a model’s direct force prediction, F̂nc , and its internally
061
      self-consistent, energy-derived force, F̂c . Using this metric, we then identify an Asymmetric Failure

062
      is a more consistent predictor of instability than the direct force error magnitude against references
063
      alone. We propose the link between this internal metric and practical reliability is a consequence
064   of inter-head influence via the shared GNN embedding, and we provide direct evidence for this
065   mechanism by demonstrating that fine-tuning the conservative force pathway measurably improves
066   the stability of simulations driven only by the direct force.

084   2    BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
085
086   Machine Learning Interatomic Potential Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials (MLIPs) aim
087   to approximate the quantum mechanical potential energy surface (PES) with the efficiency of classical
088   force fields. Early influential models were descriptor-based, first mapping local atomic environments
089   to a set of fixed, hand-crafted feature vectors (descriptors) which were then fed into a simple machine
090   learning model. Seminal examples in this class include Behler-Parrinello Neural Networks (Behler &
091
      Parrinello, 2007), Gaussian Approximation Potentials (GAP) (Bartók et al., 2010), Spectral Neighbor
      Analysis Potentials (SNAP) (Thompson et al., 2015), and Moment Tensor Potentials (MTP) (Shapeev,
092
      2016).
093
094   A subsequent generation of models moved towards end-to-end deep learning, using neural networks to
095   learn the feature representation directly from atomic coordinates. Architectures like ANI (Smith et al.,
096   2017) and SchNet (Schütt, 2017) were foundational in this area, often building upon the message-
097
      passing framework of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) (Gilmer, 2017; Battaglia, 2018). The current
      state-of-the-art is dominated by E(3)-equivariant GNNs, which build in physical symmetries (roto-
098
      translational equivariance) directly into the network architecture. This inductive bias significantly
099
      improves data efficiency and generalization (Musil, 2021). Foundational equivariant architectures
100   include Tensor Field Networks (Thomas, 2018), NequIP (Batzner et al., 2022), MACE (Batatia,
101   2022), and Allegro (Musaelian, 2023). The success of these models has spurred the development of
102   large-scale, pre-trained “foundation models” for atomistic simulation, such as CHGNet (Deng, 2023)
103   and the direct-force models used in this work.
104
105   Direct-Force Architectures. The drive for computational efficiency has popularized direct-force
106   architectures in many state-of-the-art models (Gasteiger et al., 2021; Batatia, 2022; Liao et al., 2024;
107   Neumann et al., 2024; Rhodes et al., 2025). In contrast to conservative models, these architectures
      predict atomic forces as a direct, equivariant vector output of the GNN, rather than computing


                                                          2
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




108
      them as the gradient of a predicted scalar energy. This approach can yield significant performance
109   benefits, including faster training and inference and lower memory usage, as it often avoids the
110   computational cost of a backward pass (i.e., backpropagation) through the network (Gasteiger
111   et al., 2021). Architecturally, these models typically use a shared GNN encoder to generate atomic
112   representations, which are then fed to separate output heads. The first head predicts the direct,
113   non-conservative force, F̂nc , as a direct equivariant vector output. The second head predicts a scalar
114   energy, Ê. The conservative force, F̂c , is the gradient of this energy, F̂c = −∇R Ê.
115
116   A key assumption of the direct-force paradigm is that the faster F̂nc can serve as a sufficient substitute
117   for the computationally more expensive F̂c , which requires a backward pass through the network.
118   Consequently, the conservative force pathway is typically ignored during training. The model
119
      is instead trained by minimizing a joint loss function on the energy and the direct forces, which
      encourages both accuracy on Density Functional Theory (DFT) targets and, implicitly, consistency
120
      between the two pathways:
121
122
                                  L = λE LE (Ê, EDFT ) + λF LF (F̂nc , FDFT )                              (1)
123
124
125   The Consequences of Non-Conservativity. The efficiency gain of using F̂nc comes at the expense
126   of guaranteed energy conservation (Chmiela, 2017). This lack of an underlying potential violates the
127   assumptions of algorithms that navigate the Potential Energy Surface (PES). Symplectic integrators
128   used in MD assume forces are the exact gradient of a potential to conserve the Hamiltonian (Hairer,
129   2006; Leimkuhler & Reich, 2004; Tuckerman, 2023). Non-conservative forces lead to unphysical
130
      energy drift and instabilities in NVE simulations due to its nature of not being an exact spatial gradient
      of any potential (Bigi et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024). This non-conservativity also creates artifacts
131
      in NVT simulations that are difficult or impossible to correct with thermostats without disrupting
132
      dynamical or structural properties Bigi et al. (2025). Similarly, gradient-based optimizers require a
133   consistent PES for stable convergence (Nocedal & Wright, 2006), leading to more fragile geometry
134   optimization using non-conservative forces compared to conservative forces Bigi et al. (2025).
135
136
      Requirements for Stable and Accurate MLIPs A growing body of work has established that the
137
      requirements for a reliable MLIP go far beyond simple accuracy on a static test set. The seminal
138
      work of Fu et al. (2023) provided the first large-scale benchmark demonstrating that static force error
139   is often an insufficient metric for predicting the dynamic stability of a simulation. This exposed a
140   critical gap between how models are benchmarked and how they are used in practice. However, a
141   clear and direct experimental validation of the relative importance of conservativity and accuracy
142   against a DFT reference is still lacking. By designing experiments that isolate the effects of accuracy
143   from self-consistency, we provide the first direct, quantitative evidence for the relative importance of
144   conservativity compared to accuracy.
145
146   The Limitations of Existing UQ Methods. Quantifying uncertainty is crucial for monitoring
147   reliability (Abdar et al., 2021; Musil et al., 2023). Deep ensembles remain the standard for epis-
148   temic uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), but their high computational cost (typically
149   5-10x) is prohibitive for large-scale simulations (Wen & Tadmor, 2020). Single-model Bayesian
150   approaches (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016; Vandermause et al., 2020) often require modified training.
151   Data-dependent methods (e.g., distance in latent space) (Hirschfeld et al., 2020; Podryabinkin &
152   Shapeev, 2017) are unsuitable for foundation models as they require access to massive datasets and
153   can perform poorly on heterogeneous data (Tan et al., 2023; Jablonka et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023).
154
      A fast, data-free metric derived from the model’s internal physics is needed.

      4     R ESULTS
197
198
      Our results are presented in four parts. We first experimentally establish that for stable simulations, a
199
      model’s self-consistency is more critical than its external accuracy. We then introduce the Force Delta,
200   U∆ , use it to identify the asymmetric failure mode, and validate it as a robust diagnostic for both
201   conservative and direct force errors. Building on this, we show that U∆ is a more consistent indicator
202   of algorithmic instability than standard error metrics. Finally, we provide final direct evidence for
203   the underlying inter-head mechanism by using U∆ -maximized data in fine-tuning experiments to
204   demonstrably improve model stability.

206   4.1   E XPERIMENTAL INVESTIGATION OF SIMULATION STABILITY REQUIREMENTS
207
208   We employ DFT calculations to quantitatively investigate the relative importance of accuracy against
209   FDFT compared to conservativity during the energy drift in NVE simulations. To isolate the effects of
210   accuracy from self-consistency, we perform a series of NVE simulations on a liquid water box, with
211
      consistent findings for other systems presented in the Appendix. The results, shown in Figure 1, reveal
      a clear hierarchy. First, we compare a simulation driven by the accurate but non-conservative force
212
213
      (F̂nc ) of the orb-v3-direct-inf-mpa model to one driven by its less accurate but internally
214
      self-consistent conservative force (F̂c ) obtained by backpropagating predicted energy to obtain its
215   negative spatial derivative of the same model. The F̂nc -driven simulation is unstable, while the
      F̂c -driven run is perfectly stable, providing direct proof that self-consistency (i.e., forces being an


                                                          4
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




216
      exact gradient of model’s predicted energy) is more critical than accuracy. This confirms that the
217
      small error of F̂nc against FDFT during NVE accumulates and causes the drift. No matter how close
218
      F̂nc is to an exact gradient of DFT energy (FDFT ), it will never be an exact gradient of any potential
219
      and therefore produces artifact. On the other hand, F̂c of orb-v3-direct-inf-mpa, while
220
      being inaccurate compared to FDFT , produces a stable simulations since it satisfies the symplectic
221
      requirements of the integrator by being an exact gradient of its own predicted energy. In other words,
222

      (MTS) method proposed by Bigi et al. (2025), which leverage the stability of the conservative force

      to correct the trajectory of the direct force. Furthermore, these experiments establish the scientific

253   4.2     T HE F ORCE D ELTA : A DIAGNOSTIC FOR FORCE ERRORS
254
255   Having established the requirements for simulation stability, we now validate the Force Delta, U∆ ,
256   as a diagnostic for the model properties that govern these principles. A key to understanding U∆ ’s
257   utility is the inherent asymmetry in how the two force predictions are generated and supervised.
258
259   4.2.1    T HE ASYMMETRIC FAILURE MECHANISM
260
      The dual-output architecture of direct-force models leads to a predictable, asymmetric failure mode
261
      when the model is pushed out-of-distribution (OOD). This arises from two factors: asymmetric
262
      supervision and the mathematical properties of differentiation. The direct force, F̂nc , is strongly
263
      regularized by direct supervision on vector force DFT data. In contrast, the model’s energy, Ê, is only
264
      weakly supervised by scalar values, which is insufficient to regularize the curvature of the potential
265   energy surface. Moreover, mathematically, differentiation acts as a high-pass filter, meaning that
266
      any small and high-frequency “ripples” in the under-regularized Ê (i.e., non-smooth curvature) are
267
      amplified into large-magnitude errors in its gradient, F̂c .
268
269   This mechanism predicts that as a model goes OOD, the error in the conservative force, εc , should
      grow much more rapidly than the error in the non-conservative force, εnc . This large discrepancy in


                                                         5
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




270
      error magnitudes is the key to understanding the utility of the Force Delta. Since U∆ = ∥F̂nc − F̂c ∥,
271   it can be rewritten in terms of the respective error vectors as U∆ = ∥⃗εnc − ε⃗c ∥. When the conservative
272   error dominates such that ∥⃗εc ∥ ≫ ∥⃗εnc ∥, the smaller term becomes negligible, and the expression
273   simplifies to U∆ ≈ ∥ − ε⃗c ∥ = εc . The Force Delta thus becomes a direct and precise mathematical
274   proxy for the error in the conservative force. We test this by using adversarial attacks to efficiently
275   generate OOD structures. As shown in Figure 2a, we observe a strong correlation between U∆ and
276   εc for the orb-v3-direct-20-mpa model on several crystalline systems. This result, which is
277   consistent across tested models in the Orb and EqV2 families and most systems for OOD structures
278   from both adversarial attack and high runaway temperatures during NVE (see Appendix), provides
279
      empirical evidence for the asymmetric failure mechanism and establishes U∆ as a reliable probe of
      the model’s internal physical breakdown.

303   4.2.2   F ORCE D ELTA AS A GENERAL UQ METRIC FOR FORCE ERROR
304
305   After demonstrating U∆ is a strong indicator of the model’s internal conservative force error, we now
306   investigate its utility as a practical UQ metric for the non-conservative force error that is used due
307   to its superior inference speed, εnc . A strong correlation between these two errors would suggest
308   a deeper connection between the model’s two prediction heads. We first test this relationship on a
309
      diverse benchmark set of ten systems, including crystalline solids, surface, and molecules to represent
      both in- and out-of-distribution data for the pre-trained models (see Appendix for dataset and model
310
      details). As shown in Table 1, the single-model U∆ exhibits a strong correlation with εnc , and its
311
      performance is competitive with, or superior to, the expensive multi-model ensemble variance of
312   force predictions, Uvar . It is crucial to note that these ad-hoc collection of models are not “deep
313   ensembles" in the strictest sense, as they were not co-trained with varied initializations on an identical
314   dataset. However, they represent the most direct ensemble-based UQ approach available to a user
315   working with publicly available, pre-trained models. Furthermore, combining all 12 models into
316   a single ensemble would be physically and statistically invalid. The variance would be dominated
317   by the systematic bias between the different ground-truth DFT methods used for training (DFT vs.
318   DFT+D3) rather than true epistemic uncertainty.

320   correlation on OOD structures generated via our adversarial attack. As shown for a representative
321   model in Figure 2b, U∆ maintains a strong positive correlation with εnc , even as the model is pushed
322   far from its training distribution. This result is consistent across most models and systems we tested.

355   4.3   U∆ AS A CONSISTENT INDICATOR OF ALGORITHMIC INSTABILITY
356
357   We now test the ability of U∆ to diagnose the practical consequence of these errors: algorithmic
358   instability on four systems (ice, LGPS, Mg17 Al12 , and water box). While the accumulated error,
359
      εnc , is the direct physical cause of energy drift in NVE simulations, we find its predictive power
      is complex and model-dependent. For models in a fragile state where εnc becomes large (e.g.,
360
      orb-d3-xs-v2), its magnitude correlates well with drift and all metrics (εnc , εc , U∆ , and energy
361
      drift) are well-correlated (see Appendix). However, for robust, pre-trained models, εnc often operates
362   in a low-error regime where its correlation with instability is not guaranteed.
363
364   Our results reveal a clear distinction between the predictive power of the external error magnitude
365
      and the internal inconsistency in this critical low-error regime. For strongly-regularized models like
      the OrbV3 family, the correlation between the average εnc during a simulation and the resulting
366
      energy drift is weak and noisy, as shown in Figure 3a. In contrast, Figure 3b shows that the average
367
      Force Delta, U∆ , exhibits a more consistent positive correlation with the energy drift. For other
368   models like eqV2-dens-31M-mp, all metrics happen to be well-correlated (see Appendix for a
369   full analysis). This demonstrates that while the predictive power of εnc ’s magnitude is inconsistent
370   across different model architectures and training regimes, the internal inconsistency, U∆ , is a more
371   reliable indicator of the pathological character of the error that governs the severity of the instability,
372   an artifact difficult to monitor and suppress without distrupting dynamical and structural properties in
373   NVT simulations (Bigi et al., 2025). In fact, the mean error of the conservative forces (εc ) has the
374   strongest correlation (rs = 0.97) with the energy drift in simulations performed using F̂nc . Since U∆
375   has a strong correlation with εc (also with rs = 0.97), it also predicts energy drift as well as εc . This
376   provides predictive evidence for inter-head influence, where the state of the model’s internal physics
377   and the error of the other (energy) head is a more consistent probe of its reliability than the accuracy

400   4.4    S TABILITY- IMPROVING FINETUNING EXPERIMENTS
401
402   The strong correlations observed between the model’s internal inconsistency and its practical reliability
403   suggest a deep connection between the energy and force prediction heads. We propose that this
404   inter-head influence is mediated by the shared GNN embedding. To provide direct, causal evidence
405   for this mechanism, we perform a series of fine-tuning experiments using data generated from our
406   adversarial attack on U∆ . If our hypothesis is correct, then improving the internal physics of the
407   model by training its conservative pathway should have a direct, measurable effect on the stability
408   of simulations driven only by the direct F̂nc force. Note that we have to always finetune the direct
409   force head F̂nc here to avoid degradation since the pre-trained models were trained on F̂nc and the
410   simulations are typically performed using F̂nc .
411   We test this on the orb-v3-direct-inf-mpa model with the ice system. As shown in Figure 4,
412   finetuning the model on just 100 adversarial structures leads to a clear and stepwise improvement
413   in stability. Fine-tuning only the F̂nc head reduces the energy drift compared to the pre-trained
414   baseline. Critically, fine-tuning both the F̂c and F̂nc heads (i.e., conservative fine-tuning) further
415
      reduces the drift in the F̂nc -driven simulation. This provides direct evidence that improving the
416   quality of the model’s internal energy landscape improves the non-conservative character of the direct
417   forces. Furthermore, we demonstrate the iterative utility of our method by performing a second
418   adversarial attack on this improved model to generate a new set of 100 structures. Fine-tuning on
419   this “2nd generation” data nearly eliminates the long-term energy drift. These results confirm the
420   inter-head influence mechanism and validate our method as a principled way to generate targeted
421   data for improving model’s stability.

426   Our results, from the error correlations to finetuning experiments, all point to a single unifying
427   mechanism: inter-head influence via the shared GNN embedding. The state of the model’s internal
428   physics, represented by F̂c , is not independent of the direct force prediction, F̂nc . This finding
429   is consistent with and provides a deeper explanation for previous observations that pre-training a
430   model on its direct-force head provides a more effective starting point for subsequently training the
431   conservative-force pathway (Bigi et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2024). Our work reveals both the diagnostic
      and improvement sides of this phenomenon: the observable state of one head is a sensitive probe

459   This understanding establishes the Force Delta, U∆ , which measures the disagreement between two

461
      the two forces do not agree, at least one of them is wrong against DFT, then the error of the other
      force could also be large in magnitude as shown by correlational evidence in Section 4.2.2, or could
462

463
      4.3 or both. It serves as an on-the-fly monitor for MD simulations (both NVE and NVT) to detect the
464   onset of non-conservative artifacts that can corrupt dynamical properties. For geometry optimizations
465   and property predictions, it acts as a fast, data-free prerequisite check on the trustworthiness of the
466   underlying PES. Furthermore, as our fine-tuning experiments demonstrate, it provides a data-efficient
467   method for generating targeted OOD structures to improve the stability of both pre-trained and already
468   fine-tuned models.
469
      The demonstrated stability improvements from our fine-tuning workflow have direct implications for
470   advanced simulation methods. The Multiple-Time-Stepping (MTS) scheme proposed by Bigi et al.
471
      (2025), for instance, relies on the accuracy of both the direct force F̂nc and the corrective conservative
472
      force F̂c . Our work establishes the Force Delta, U∆ , as an essential real-time monitor for this scheme,
473
      as a large U∆ signals that at least one of these forces has become unreliable. Furthermore, by using
474   our method to create a more stable base model, we can logically infer that the MTS algorithm
475   would require less frequent corrective steps. This would lead to a significant increase in the overall
476   simulation speed without sacrificing stability, directly addressing a key challenge in the field.
477
      Finally, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations of this approach, which also point to future
478
      directions. Due to its disagreement-based nature, U∆ cannot detect “consensus failures” where both
479
      force predictions are concurrently wrong, a rare but possible scenario we observed for a specific
480   model-system pair (MoF5) in the Appendix. Furthermore, in the highly consistent, low-error regime,
481   the magnitude of all metrics (U∆ , εc , and εnc ) is small, and correlations with instability may be
482   dominated by numerical noise; differentiating between near-zero uncertainty and noise remains
483   a challenge for any metric. Lastly, U∆ is suitable for ranking uncertainty, which is the primary
484   requirement for failure detection and active learning. However, it is not calibrated; the magnitude of
485   U∆ is not a direct predictor of the magnitude of εnc . Calibrated error estimation would still require
      system-specific validation (Kuleshov et al., 2018) and represents a key area for future work.
